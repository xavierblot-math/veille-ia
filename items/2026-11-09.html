Proofs and Prompts (Filippo A. E. Nuccio) — Formalisation as a concrete manifestation of mathematical understanding

  • A Mathlib maintainer (Université Saint-Étienne) argues that formalising mathematics by hand is a way to turn the vague notion of "understanding" into a tangible, checkable scientific output, largely independent of AI.
  • He draws a parallel with open access: blaming publishers (or AI companies) is a distraction when the community's own reward system (publication counts, rankings) undervalues time spent understanding.
  • Formalising is not mechanical: it means recasting definitions and proofs into better-suited forms, a collective effort (Mathlib, ~2.5M lines, hundreds of contributors); he cites the aims of the Annals of Formalized Mathematics, which he co-founded with Rob Lewis.
  • Autoformalisation by LLMs is to human-built libraries what bookbinding is to the printing press: it relies on Mathlib rather than replacing it. Refers to Gowers' recent text on the many purposes of mathematics.
  • 👉 French mathematical community voice (also active in MathOA and the French Committee for Open Science); relevant to how to frame PhD students' work.

Terence Tao (guest post: Jana Gevertz & Steffen Marcus) — What should we teach our (undergraduate) students?

  • Follow-up to Lozano-Robledo's "what should we tell our students?": two faculty at a New Jersey liberal arts college ask how undergraduate curricula should change now that AI turns advanced coursework into a push-button exercise.
  • Core tension: understanding comes from productive struggle (e.g. finding the right substitution yourself), yet students must learn to check and interpret AI output; both "ban it" and "rebuild everything around it" are rejected.
  • They pose open questions: more emphasis on conceptual reasoning and modelling (per the MAA statement on AI), critical evaluation of AI output, Lean in proof-based courses, fewer topics, coordination with other disciplines; and call for AMS/MAA leadership and federal grants.

Proofs and Prompts (Sajad Ahmad Sheikh) — Sisyphus and the Sorcerer: An Existential Meditation at the Chalkboard

  • A personal essay (University of Kashmir) recasting Camus' Sisyphus: mathematicians once formed a village whose stones stayed up; AI is a "sorcerer" (a golem we built) lifting ever-heavier stones.
  • Past shocks (Gödel, the four-colour proof, the classification of finite simple groups) kept understanding with humans; the worry now, following Thurston, is whether anyone will be able to say why a theorem is true.
  • He questions the usual reassurances (humans will choose the questions; maths as a hobby like chess) and names the new threat as superfluity rather than futility, ending on an honest "not proven".
