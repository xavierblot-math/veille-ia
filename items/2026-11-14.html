Terence Tao (blog, guest post) — Introducing MathHelm: A Place to discuss AI-generated papers

  • A committee of mathematicians announces MathHelm, a nonprofit forum for discussing large AI-generated paper collections, starting with the 722 manuscripts OpenAI released on 6 October 2026, many of them without Lean verification.
  • Researchers register under their real names and comment on correctness, readability, originality or key ideas, one step or reference at a time; PDF notes can be uploaded. Forum content may not be used to train AI models, and the forum takes no stance for or against AI.
  • Guiding idea: nobody is obliged to check AI papers, but partial observations from anyone who reads part of one are worth collecting in one place rather than scattered online.
  • Committee: Xinyi Yuan (Peking, chair), Yi Ni (Caltech), Xiaoming Sun (ICT–CAS), Liang Xiao (Peking), Yuan Yuan (Westlake), Ruixiang Zhang (Berkeley), Fan Zheng (ICMAT–CSIC).
  • 👉 A practical place to check community feedback on any of the 722 papers in your area before relying on them or pointing a student to one.

Proofs and Prompts (Benjamin Friedman) — Human skepticism and formal verification

  • A UQÀM postdoc argues that Lean mechanizes the skeptic's side of the prover–skeptic dialogue (drawing on Dutilh Novaes and Bessis's Mathematica), but a Lean certificate cannot replace personal, methodological skepticism.
  • He walks through a small Lean proof that √2 is irrational, where Lean rejects "gcd = 1" in place of Mathlib's ideal-theoretic "coprime": a fussy audience member in effect.
  • He reads OpenAI's speed as both roles being automated (LLM prover, Lean skeptic) and doubts fields without a verifier, such as oncology, will see similar gains soon. He also criticises the papers being credited simply to "OpenAI" and called solved on release day.
  • Even granting a perfect kernel, trusting a certificate is an appeal to authority; understanding comes from testing and correcting one's own intuition, and nothing can do that for you.

Proofs and Prompts (Florian Breuer) — A vision of the future of mathematics

  • A Newcastle (Australia) professor takes superhuman AI mathematics as a given fact and predicts the research frontier becomes a fast-growing formal (Lean-like) "monolith" beyond human understanding, with human mathematics turning recreational, like childhood maths.
  • He sees the real danger as handing over control domain by domain until humanity is gradually disempowered, so this good outcome depends on solving AI alignment.
  • Short term: research time is hard to justify, and a thesis is worth the skills it builds, not its results. He is retraining in AI safety and lists entry points for mathematicians (BlueDot, MAISI, Iliad, Lionel Levine's note). Acknowledges David Harvey and Fabien Pazuki.
  • 👉 Directly addresses PhD supervision: what a thesis is for when results are cheap.

Proofs and Prompts (Advith Govindarajan) — Positivist and Humanist Mathematics: A Case for Schism

  • A UIUC PhD student argues mathematics should formally split into a "positivist" discipline (producing theorems, now largely by machine) and a "humanist" one (beauty and understanding as ends), each with its own journals, jobs and norms.
  • Letting humans formulate theories and machines prove them already concedes the positivist view, he says; humanist mathematics is closer to painting after photography.
  • His test case is a hiring choice between 30 rough, LLM-heavy papers with major results and 5 beautifully written human papers on small questions. In his view, the answer shows which discipline you belong to, and without a deliberate split one side will crowd out the other.

Terence Tao (blog) — 247A, Notes 4: Weighted estimates and pseudodifferential operators

  • Lecture notes for his harmonic analysis course. They prove that weighted bounds are equivalent to vector-valued estimates using Hahn–Banach, and that for linear operators they are also equivalent to higher unweighted estimates.
  • They then prove a weighted Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality by adapting the unweighted Vitali covering proof, before moving on to pseudodifferential operators.
