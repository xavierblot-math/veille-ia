Terence Tao (guest post by Tristan Buckmaster) — Mathematics is a subject for the young

  • Expands his 19 October IAS talk. He returns to his "Deep Blue–Kasparov moment" comparison (made when he and Alpöge released their Euler result): OpenAI's Navier–Stokes proof reportedly took 10,000 agents running for 88 hours, a scale that is out of reach today but that falling costs could soon make widely available.
  • He argues that racing to be first on famous problems is over, but mathematics is not, because its value lies in human understanding: a machine-certified proof of RH would still leave open why it is true.
  • He sees mathematical physics as a natural target for AI: making heuristic physics rigorous, as Deng–Hani(–Ma) did for wave kinetic theory and for the Boltzmann equation (Hilbert's sixth problem), could become feasible for other effective theories.
  • Sharp criticism of AI companies: competing with their own customers, the 6 October OpenAI manuscript dump (some proofs unreadable or false, misleading compute claims, possible overlap with unreleased student work). He proposes zero-retention enterprise agreements covering every academic, opt-in-only training on user data, open-weight infrastructure, and a credit system that rewards exposition, disclosure and shared tools.
  • 👉 Directly relevant: AI and mathematical physics, plus concrete advice on data protection and credit that applies to PhD supervision.

Proofs and Prompts (Yiannis Sakellaridis) — Paralysis

  • He compares AI to a powerful new telescope for exploring mathematical truth, but warns against the alternative story in which machines do all the understanding, which in practice would mean subordination to the companies that own them.
  • He argues that AI firms need to capture scientific output as intellectual property to pay back trillions in investment, after training on publicly funded work released under non-commercial licences, and that their opacity makes "training off" settings impossible to verify.
  • His main charge is against the community: professional societies, institutions and advisory bodies have stayed silent on misconduct and IP theft, which he calls a "mathematical community in a state of paralysis". He also calls for public AI infrastructure for science.

Proofs and Prompts (Pavel Etingof & Slava Gerovitch, MIT) — Mathematics of the Future

  • Part of the 19–23 October series for PhD students and postdocs; a summary of a longer text. They argue that the four functions of mathematics (truth, culture, applications, intellectual growth) all survive AI, and that society should maintain mathematical expertise the way it maintains fire departments.
  • They expect the priority-based "ownership culture" to give way to evaluation based on intellectual agency: hiring through talks and direct conversation, and treating AI-assisted theorems the same as unassisted ones.
  • New roles they foresee: interpreting massive AI output, a "big cleanup" of the literature (fixing errors and gaps, plus Lean formalization alongside readable exposition), and richer publication formats. Teaching would add oral exams and value breadth over early specialization; a thesis need not contain a first proof.
  • Concrete proposal: small faculty-led labs where a group brainstorms, the AI responds to the notes, and students check and discuss the answers in a shared document. Advice to the young: accept uncertainty, learn broadly, collaborate.
  • 👉 Directly about PhD training and supervision; the lab model is worth reading in full.

Proofs and Prompts (Márton Balázs, Bristol) — The Power of Beauty and Elegance

  • He frames the "Ultimate Question" as whether brute knowledge and compute can beat human intuition for beauty and elegance in every area of mathematics.
  • He points out that most guidance on human-scale mathematics quietly assumes the answer is no. If it is yes, the profession becomes an exotic art with little funding, and the same would hold across science and engineering.
  • He remains optimistic, at least for many years, because LLMs still seem far from a useful sense of elegance for finding their way among possible theorems.
