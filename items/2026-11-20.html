Proofs and Prompts (José Fernández Tamames) — Who answers for a proof? What the person who presents it commits to, even once it is verified

  • A philosopher of technology and law (UNIE, Madrid) responds to Tao's ICM lecture on what mathematics is for, accepting its working hypothesis that AI will soon do a sizable share of research-level work.
  • He endorses Tao's rule that a result shouldn't be published unless its authors can give a clear expert talk on it, but argues the rule leaves open what presenting a result actually commits someone to.
  • He imports a three-part accountability test from legal theory (with Susana Checa Prieto): having been able to refrain, giving case-specific reasons, and having something personally at stake — conditions he says current AI systems don't meet in their own right.
  • Key point: after formal verification, correctness no longer depends on the author; what remains tied to the person is explaining and defending the result, so tools should be designed to make that possible.

Proofs and Prompts (Gaia Carenini) — The Right to Think

  • A Cambridge PhD student in mathematics writes a personal essay on the risk of delegating the struggle of not-understanding, which she sees as where mathematical learning actually happens.
  • She doesn't dismiss AI results (citing the May 2026 OpenAI counterexample on Erdős unit distances and the October batch of model-generated manuscripts), but asks how young mathematicians will develop taste and judgment if they can skip every difficulty.
  • Also raises collective effects: output-per-time becoming a hiring metric, paid tools widening inequalities between countries, and dependence on commercial providers.
  • 👉 Directly relevant to PhD supervision — a doctoral student's view of what training should protect.
