Terence Tao's blog (guest post by Frank Calegari) — Mathematics has changed. Let’s act like it

  • An optimistic response to the gloom after OpenAI's October 6 release of 700+ AI papers: those papers are badly written, often misattributed and sometimes wrong, but the community controls hiring, publishing and training, and should act there rather than lament.
  • Draws on the classification of finite simple groups as a precedent: solving a field's "famous problem" can chill it, so the real work is revision, digestion and refusing to declare subjects finished (cites Duminil-Copin's reaction that all his open problems were claimed at once).
  • Argues AI lowers the barrier to speculative, cross-field programs; his own example links compact arithmetic hyperbolic 3-manifolds, Bloch groups and a hoped-for integral Jacquet–Langlands / Kolyvagin-system picture.
  • Concrete proposals: judge every paper as if AI-assisted (value only what a machine couldn't trivially produce), interview postdoc and PhD candidates in person, demand more of journals or abandon them, broaden graduate training, give context-first seminars, and "take ownership" of AI results on behalf of humanity.
  • 👉 Directly relevant to PhD supervision: he says the classic "problem in the overhang" thesis is now a button-press, so training must emphasise breadth and the ability to explain mathematics.

Proofs and Prompts (Alexander Van Werde) — Collaborative work groups on exposition problems

  • Part of the publishing/credit series. Worry: AI makes first proofs cheap but expertise is still needed to digest and write them up, and a race to post the first acceptable exposition could replicate scooping pressure.
  • Proposal: openly announced "collaborative work groups" (not necessarily led by the original author) that digest an AI-generated preprint, publish partial reports on a blog or Git repo, and aim for a human-written paper submittable to classical venues.
  • Rationale: individual ownership feels odd for autonomous AI output, groups spread the ideas, and announcing protects a slow digestion process; he flags the risk of legitimising badly written preprints.

Proofs and Prompts (Andrew Ng, Bonn) — A new system of accreditation

  • A PhD student proposes abolishing refereed journals (no refereeing for submissions after 1 January 2029) in favour of thesis-scale "publications" defended publicly once a year, administered centrally (e.g. by the IMU), with a named examiner and signed community reviews.
  • arXiv results stay the author's to defend for two years, then become "folklore" usable by anyone who digests them — e.g. a cleaned-up rewrite of an AI company's 100-page preprint would earn the credit.
  • Also argues for credit for outreach, public/open LLMs allocated like supercomputer time, and draws lessons from chess training; discusses drawbacks (subjectivity, burden on examiners, small results) and offers his own 2027 defence as a test run.
  • 👉 Mentions CNRS's reported €3M in 2025 publishing fees, a point for the French debate.
