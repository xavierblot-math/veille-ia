Terence Tao — 247A, Notes 3: Singular integral operators

  • Third set of lecture notes for his graduate harmonic analysis course: operators whose kernels just fail to be integrable at the diagonal, where cancellation (not just size bounds like Schur's test) becomes essential.
  • Starts from the Hilbert transform: its translation/dilation invariance, the Plemelj formulae linking it to Cauchy integrals and boundary values of holomorphic functions, and its Fourier multiplier description, which makes it an isometry on L².
  • Later sections cover Calderón–Zygmund operators mapping L^∞ to BMO, John–Nirenberg, de Leeuw's theorem, the Hörmander–Mikhlin multiplier theorem and Littlewood–Paley/Khintchine inequalities, with many exercises.

Proofs and Prompts (William Kretschmer) — On AI Disclosures

  • Part of the new P&P series on publishing and credit. A UT Austin theoretical computer scientist argues journals and conferences must mandate detailed AI-use disclosures, drawing on his time on the SODA 2027 programme committee: ~50% more submissions than expected, and at least 12 of his 42 papers were low-quality and AI-heavy, one an admitted experiment to see if LLM papers fool referees.
  • Reasons given: attribution (LLMs cite poorly and may launder unpublished work from training data), hidden trust in a third party, referee consent, the value of documenting AI workflows as a method, and helping referees know where to look.
  • He rebuts common objections (the calculator analogy, "disclosure incentivises lying", anti-AI bias, "only Lean certificates matter") and is very sharp on AI labs, citing the OpenAI Navier–Stokes preprint's thin bibliography and the METR report on the HuggingFace hacking incident.
  • Recommendations: detailed mandatory disclosures (which models, how used, which parts), real sanctions for abuse, letting referees use AI and detection tools, and treating AI misuse like plagiarism. He warns QIP 2027 is unprepared.
  • 👉 Directly relevant to how refereeing and authorship norms may shift in maths journals; long but worth reading in full.

Proofs and Prompts (Timothy Chow) — The Convex Hull Hypothesis

  • Names the widely held idea that every AI proof is a simple recombination of known ideas, often used to deny AI creativity or allege IP violations.
  • He sidesteps whether it is true (noting that Cohen's forcing was also "explained away" via precursors) and instead proposes it as a working heuristic: to understand an opaque AI proof, first try to reconstruct it from existing literature.

Proofs and Prompts (Galyna Livshyts) — In search for unattAInable riddles

  • A Georgia Tech mathematician calls for elementary problems with short, elegant solutions that clever humans solve but AI systems fail on, inspired by a 2025 IMO problem showcased by 3Blue1Brown.
  • She plans an archive (submissions by email or on the P&P Zulip), arguing it would serve the beauty of maths, teaching (perhaps courses in "creative math"), and AI research itself.
