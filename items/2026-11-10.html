Proofs and Prompts (Juan Souto) — If Life Gives You Lemons…

  • A deliberately colloquial anti-doom rant (originally an e-mail, written without AI) by a CNRS directeur de recherche at IRMAR, Rennes: the panic among mathematicians mixes up separate questions (jobs, devalued papers, students, fun) that should be pulled apart.
  • Jobs: mathematicians are funded for teaching and for the occasional economically valuable spin-off, not cultural prestige; a faster research tool is an argument for more funding, and the real risk is mathematicians loudly declaring the field over.
  • Hiring must stop counting papers, but a week of talking to two people still reveals who is the better mathematician. PhD topics of the "solid problem that surely yields something" type are now five-minute AI tasks, so he admits he would not know what to give a new student.
  • His own practice: think as if AI didn't exist, then let it "break stones" on the painful lemma once he understands why it holds. His main worry is that the community stops sharing open questions, now that "questions are the new gold".
  • 👉 A French voice speaking directly to PhD supervision and to anxious postdocs (MPI, IHES and the rest will keep hiring for now).

Proofs and Prompts (Senthil Rajasekaran) — The Bigger Picture

  • A TCS postdoc (ULB) whose conceptual, definition-driven style should make him the most welcoming of theorem-provers argues that mathematics was not singled out but was a convenient "target of opportunity" for demonstrating AGI-style capabilities.
  • He calls it a hostile move: the goal of AI companies is replacing human labour at scale, not augmenting it, and mathematicians should ally with translators, artists and laid-off workers rather than seek special sympathy.
  • Ends with an explicitly political call to defend the meaning of human effort before trying to "take back control" of the field.

Proofs and Prompts (Antonio Lerario) — The shape of mathematical knowledge

  • A SISSA professor's philosophical essay built on three principles: science should serve human life; AI contributions should be judged on their merits rather than excluded for being artificial; human participation should be kept as a deliberate choice.
  • Main proposal: a shared, persistent "epistemic object", a layered hypergraph with a formalized core (definitions, proofs, dependencies, provenance) plus semantic layers for explanation, with Stacks Project, Verso Blueprint, MathHub and TheoremGraph cited as predecessors. It would pay off the corpus's accumulated "epistemic debt" and keep a flood of new results usable.
  • Research would be organised into long collective programmes that outlive their founders. Education should find the formative difficulties worth preserving, and evaluation should reward exposition, connections and good questions over priority and "theorem ownership". He also suggests departments run local models.
  • 👉 Worth reading in full: a constructive structural vision rather than a reaction piece.

Terence Tao (guest post) — Our Survey of Early Career Mathematicians

  • Anna Brandenberger, Hanna Mularczyk and Dora Woodruff (MIT) present an informal survey of over 260 grad students and postdocs from about 60 institutions on AI, with results posted on the arXiv on 29 October.
  • The authors highlight the questions on career outlook and research motivation as the most striking, and note that most responses predate 6 October, so opinion may have shifted since.
  • They urge journals, hiring committees and faculty to take junior voices into account.
  • 👉 Relevant for PhD supervision: the post itself is short, but the linked results are the substance.
